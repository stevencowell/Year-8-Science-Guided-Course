Y8S-M01 · Data science 1 - part A

M01-S01

Data sources, digital traces and safe engagement

Where scientific data comes from

Scientific data can come from many places. Scientists may collect first-hand data themselves through observations, measurements, sensors or experiments. For example, a researcher might record the number of birds seen at several wetlands or use a sensor to record air temperature. First-hand data is collected directly for the investigation being carried out. Secondary-source data has already been collected by someone else and is reused for a new investigation. A public weather dataset, for instance, can be analysed to look for patterns across seasons even though the person analysing it did not make the original measurements.

A dataset is an organised collection of values or observations used to investigate a question. It may contain numbers, categories, dates, locations described at a broad level, or other clearly defined fields. Good datasets make it easier to compare observations and look for patterns. Data science brings scientific thinking together with mathematics and computational tools to help organise, examine and interpret this information. A computer can sort or graph thousands of records quickly, but the quality of the conclusion still depends on whether the data is suitable for the question. A large dataset is not automatically a useful dataset. Scientists still need to ask where the data came from, what was measured, and whether important information is missing.

Digital traces and privacy

Online activity and connected devices can create digital traces. A digital trace is information left behind when a device, system or online service records an action or event. For example, a networked weather sensor might store the time and temperature of each reading. A website may also record that a page was visited or that a button was selected. These traces can be useful because many small records can reveal patterns that would be hard to notice one at a time.

Digital traces need careful handling because some can expose personal or sensitive information. A record that includes a name, exact location, contact detail or another identifying feature could reveal more than is needed for a scientific question. Even when one field looks harmless by itself, several fields combined may make a person easier to identify. For this reason, scientific investigations should use non-identifying or appropriately authorised data and should include only the information needed for the purpose. Hypothetical examples such as public weather observations, animal counts or de-identified sensor readings are safer for learning because they allow patterns to be explored without using personal information.

Provenance and safe use

Data provenance is the story of where a dataset came from and what happened to it before it was analysed. Useful provenance information includes who or what collected the data, how it was collected, when it was collected, what each field means, and whether any values were changed, removed or combined. It also includes known limits. For example, an animal-count dataset may have missing days because no observations were recorded, or a sensor dataset may use different sampling times in different periods. Knowing these details helps a scientist decide whether comparisons are fair.

Safe scientific engagement means more than simply avoiding names. It includes respecting privacy and consent, checking who is allowed to access the data, considering how the data is stored or shared, and recognising when a dataset is biased or incomplete. Bias can occur when some groups, places or times are represented more strongly than others. Incomplete data can hide important variation. If a dataset contains only summer weather observations, it would be weak evidence for a claim about the whole year. Careful scientists therefore check provenance before drawing conclusions and clearly describe limits when reporting patterns. This does not make the data useless; it makes the interpretation more accurate and responsible.

M01-S02

Scientific questions and collecting datasets

Building useful scientific questions

A useful scientific question gives scientists a clear direction. It should be specific enough that observations or data could help answer it. Questions such as “Which month is better?” depend on opinion, so they are not strong scientific questions. A stronger question asks about a relationship, comparison or pattern, such as whether one weather variable tends to change when another changes, or whether animal counts differ between two locations in an existing dataset.

A good question also makes it easier to decide what information is needed. Before looking at a dataset, scientists can ask: What will be compared? What might change? What will be measured or recorded? What pattern would the data need to show? This planning helps stop a dataset from becoming a collection of unrelated values.

Scientific questions do not need to predict the answer. Their purpose is to guide an investigation fairly and clearly. A question can be useful even when the final data show little difference or no obvious relationship. The important point is that the question can be addressed using suitable observations or data rather than personal preference.

Scientists may also refine a question after noticing that it is too broad. For example, “How does weather change?” could refer to many variables and many time periods. A more focused question could compare one weather measure across clearly defined categories or periods in a public dataset. Keeping the question focused makes later decisions about variables, fields and data quality much easier.

Variables, data types and dataset structure

A variable is a factor that can change, be measured or be kept consistent. In a fair comparison, the independent variable is the factor deliberately changed, while the dependent variable is the factor measured to see whether it responds. Relevant controlled variables are kept consistent so that the comparison is easier to interpret. In an existing observational dataset, scientists may instead compare variables that were recorded without deliberately changing them.

Before data are collected or selected, scientists decide what type of data each field will contain. Numerical data use numbers that represent measurements or counts. Categorical data place records into named groups or categories. A dataset may contain both. Clear field names are important because a label such as “temperature” is less useful than a label that also makes the meaning and unit clear.

Units or categories need to stay consistent across records. Mixing metres and centimetres in one field, or changing category labels part-way through a dataset, can cause confusion and errors. Scientists also think about a suitable range and frequency. The range is the span of values, locations, groups or times needed to address the question. Frequency describes how often records occur or observations are represented.

Metadata provide information needed to interpret a record. Useful metadata can explain what each field means, which units or categories are used, when the record relates to, where the data came from, and whether anything changed. Missing values should also be recorded consistently rather than being silently replaced or ignored. These choices make datasets easier to combine, compare and understand.

Collecting data you can trust

Good datasets are planned for consistency. Scientists aim to use the same field names, units, categories and recording intervals wherever records are meant to be compared. Consistency does not guarantee that the data are useful, but it reduces avoidable confusion. If one part of a dataset uses different labels or intervals, that difference should be clear in the metadata.

Repeated observations or measurements help scientists see natural variation. One value may be unusually high or low, while repeated values show whether that result is typical. Repetition can therefore support more reliable comparisons. For numerical data, the mean can be used to describe an average value when that summary is appropriate. The range shows the spread from the lowest value to the highest value. These summaries can help describe a dataset, but they do not replace the original records.

Reliability and validity describe different qualities. Reliability is about consistency across repeated observations or measurements. If repeated records vary greatly for no clear reason, reliability may be a concern. Validity is about whether the data and the way they were produced actually address the intended scientific question. A dataset could be very consistent but still be unsuitable for the question being asked.

Before using a dataset, scientists therefore check more than whether it contains enough rows. They consider whether the variables match the question, whether the fields and units are consistent, whether repetitions are adequate, and whether the metadata explain where the records came from and how to interpret them. A well-planned dataset is easier to inspect, summarise and compare, and it gives stronger support for scientific conclusions.

M01-S03

Analysing datasets, patterns and solutions

Representing and comparing data

Scientists often begin analysis by organising data so that values can be compared clearly. A table is useful for keeping individual values in order, while a graph or other representation can make differences and patterns easier to see. The most suitable representation depends on the type of data and the question being investigated. A useful graph needs enough information for another reader to understand what is being shown. This usually includes a clear title, labelled axes, units where they are needed, a sensible scale and a brief source note when the data came from somewhere else. These features matter because a graph can be technically neat but still be difficult or misleading to interpret if its labels or scale are unclear.

Summary measures can support comparisons between groups of numerical values. The mean can be used to describe a typical value, while the range describes the spread between the lowest and highest values. Two datasets can have similar means but very different ranges, so the spread of values can change how a comparison is interpreted. A mean can also be affected by an unusually high or low value. For this reason, scientists do not rely on one summary number alone.

Good analysis keeps the individual values visible as well as any calculated summaries. Before making a claim, scientists compare the table, representation and summary measures together. They also check whether values are missing and whether the source of the data is known. Organising data carefully does not create new evidence; it helps the evidence already present in the dataset become easier to inspect and compare.

Patterns, outliers and conclusions

A pattern is a feature that appears across a dataset rather than in only one isolated value. Patterns can include repeated features, an overall increase or decrease, or a relationship between two variables. Scientists look for patterns because they can help answer the original question, but they also check whether the pattern is strong enough to support a conclusion. An outlier is a value that is noticeably different from the rest. It may be important, so it should be checked rather than automatically removed. The value could reflect a real difference, a recording problem or something not yet understood.

A conclusion should directly answer the question being investigated. It should refer to a pattern, comparison or summary measure as evidence rather than simply stating an opinion. A strong conclusion also recognises important limits in the data. For example, a small dataset, missing values, uncertain provenance or disagreement with reliable observations can reduce confidence in a conclusion. Scientists consider reliability, validity and provenance when judging data quality. Reliability is about whether information is dependable and reasonably consistent. Validity is about whether the data are suitable for answering the question. Provenance helps a reader understand where the data came from, what the values mean and whether any important changes or limits are known.

Data can support or refute a prediction or proposed explanation, but a shared pattern does not automatically show cause. If two variables rise or fall together, they may be correlated, but another variable could be influencing both. Scientists may need additional evidence before claiming that one variable caused another. Careful conclusions therefore separate what the data clearly show from what still remains uncertain.

Evaluating and justifying solutions

Data analysis can also support problem-solving. Scientists first identify the problem clearly so they know what needs to improve. They can then compare possible strategies using stated criteria. Criteria are the features used to judge the options, such as how well each strategy is expected to address the problem, how practical it is to apply, or how well it fits the available evidence. The important point is that the criteria are stated before a choice is justified, so the decision is not based only on personal preference.

A suitable solution is not simply the option that sounds most impressive. It is the option that best matches the evidence and the chosen criteria. For example, a hypothetical dataset might show that one location has consistently higher values than others. Several possible strategies could be proposed, but the preferred strategy should target the pattern shown in the data and meet the agreed criteria. If the dataset has important limitations, the justification should recognise them. A solution can be reasonable even when the evidence is not perfect, as long as the limits are made clear and the choice is not presented as certain.

Scientists also compare their interpretation with other reliable information when it is available. Agreement with observations or reliable secondary sources can increase confidence, while disagreement is a reason to investigate further. Missing values, weak provenance or an outlier can also affect how strongly a solution can be justified. A careful problem-solving statement therefore explains the problem, compares options, names the selected solution and links that choice to evidence and criteria. It does not claim that the chosen solution is guaranteed to work. Instead, it shows why the solution is the most suitable choice based on the information currently available.

Y8S-M02 · Living systems

M02-S01

Human body systems

Levels of organisation

A multicellular organism is made of many cells, but those cells do not all have the same job. Specialised cells have features that help them carry out particular functions. Groups of similar or related specialised cells can work together as tissues. Different tissues can then combine to form an organ, and organs can work together as an organ system. This organisation can be shown as a sequence: specialised cells → tissues → organs → organ systems → organism.

Each level depends on the levels below it. A tissue can only do its job because its cells are working together. An organ combines different tissues so that several related tasks can happen in one structure. An organ system combines organs that contribute to a larger body function. The human body is therefore not a collection of separate parts. It is a set of connected structures whose functions depend on coordinated activity.

Structure and function are linked. A structure is the form or arrangement of a body part, while function is what that part does. Some body surfaces are thin, which can support exchange. Some surfaces are folded, increasing the area available for absorption. Blood vessels branch so transport can reach many parts of the body. Specialised transport cells in blood help move substances from place to place. These examples show that body structures are suited to the work they perform.

Diagrams and other representations are useful because they help us organise these relationships clearly. An annotated diagram can label important parts and add short notes about function or connection. However, a diagram is always a simplified representation. It can show the main ideas without showing every structure, every cell or every process happening in the real body.

Major body systems

The digestive system changes food into smaller substances that can be absorbed and used by the body. Major parts include the mouth, oesophagus, stomach, small intestine and large intestine. The liver and pancreas also contribute to digestion. At Year 8 level, the key idea is that food is broken down, useful substances are absorbed, and the remaining material continues through the system. Folded absorptive surfaces help make absorption effective by providing a larger area where substances can move into transport pathways.

The respiratory system includes airways and lungs. Its main role is gas exchange. Oxygen from inhaled air moves into the body, while carbon dioxide moves out. Thin exchange surfaces support this movement because substances can cross a short distance between air spaces and nearby transport pathways. The respiratory system does not work alone; the oxygen it supplies must be moved to cells elsewhere in the body.

The circulatory system uses the heart, blood and blood vessels to transport substances. The heart drives the movement of blood, while vessels form branching pathways throughout the body. Blood carries oxygen, absorbed nutrients and wastes. This transport role links the circulatory system closely with the digestive and respiratory systems because materials entering the body must be delivered to cells.

The excretory system includes the kidneys and associated urinary structures. Its role includes removing dissolved metabolic wastes from the blood and helping regulate water balance. This system contributes to maintaining suitable internal conditions by separating some waste substances from useful materials and water. These four systems have different main functions, but they operate as parts of one larger organism.

How body systems work together

Body systems are interdependent, which means they rely on one another. Cells need a continuing supply of useful materials and they also produce wastes. The digestive system supplies absorbed nutrients. The respiratory system supplies oxygen and removes carbon dioxide from gas exchange surfaces. The circulatory system connects these sources with cells by transporting substances through blood vessels. Excretory processes remove dissolved metabolic wastes and help regulate water balance.

A simple pathway can be followed. Nutrients are absorbed from the digestive system into transport pathways. Oxygen moves from the lungs into the body. Blood then carries oxygen and nutrients through branching vessels so they can reach cells. Cells use materials for their activities and produce wastes. Carbon dioxide is transported towards the respiratory system so it can leave the body, while some dissolved metabolic wastes are transported towards the kidneys for removal. This sequence shows how several systems contribute to one connected process.

Because systems are linked, a disease or disorder affecting one component can have effects beyond that single part. In a hypothetical example, suppose a component of a transport system is not able to perform its usual function effectively. The system may then transport substances less effectively, which can also affect connected systems that rely on that transport and reduce the organism's ability to maintain normal functioning. This is a systems explanation rather than a diagnosis: the key idea is that disruption at one level can affect a component, its organ system, connected systems and the whole organism.

An annotated four-system representation can make these connections easier to explain. Labels might identify a system or major structure, while arrows can show the direction that materials move. Short annotations can explain what is being transported, supplied or removed. The representation should still be treated as a model rather than a complete picture of the body. Real body systems have many more structures and simultaneous interactions than a simple Year 8 diagram can show.

M02-S02

Plant systems

Plant organs working together

A multicellular plant is made of many cells organised into tissues and organs. Roots, stems and leaves are different organs with distinct roles, but they depend on one another. A plant functions as a connected system rather than as a collection of separate parts. For example, water absorbed by roots can be transported through the stem to leaves, where it contributes to photosynthesis. Sugars formed in leaves can then be transported to other parts of the plant that use or store them.

Roots help anchor a plant and absorb water and dissolved mineral ions. Some cells in roots are specialised for this role. Root-hair cells have an extended surface, which supports the absorption of water and dissolved mineral ions from around the roots. Their structure is therefore linked to their function: the extended surface provides more area through which absorption can occur.

Stems support leaves and connect the plant's transport tissues between roots and shoots. This connection is important because leaves need materials supplied from other parts of the plant, while other organs can depend on sugars made in leaves. Leaves are shaped to capture light and exchange gases. These roles support photosynthesis, the process in which light energy is used to form sugars from carbon dioxide and water, with oxygen released. Looking at roots, stems and leaves together helps explain why damage or reduced function in one organ can affect the whole plant system.

Specialised cells and transport tissues

Within plant organs, specialised cells and tissues carry out particular functions. In leaves, palisade cells contain many chloroplasts. Chloroplasts are associated with photosynthesis, so having many chloroplasts supports the role of palisade cells in sugar production when light, carbon dioxide and water are available. This is an example of a structure-function relationship: a feature of the cell helps it perform a particular role in the plant.

Leaves also contain stomata and guard cells. Stomata are openings involved in gas exchange, while guard cells regulate these openings. Together they help control the movement of gases and the loss of water from the leaf. This matters because carbon dioxide is needed for photosynthesis, while water is also required by the plant. Gas exchange and water loss therefore need to be considered as linked parts of leaf function rather than as completely separate processes.

Two important transport tissues are xylem and phloem. Xylem transports water and dissolved minerals mainly from the roots through the plant. Phloem transports dissolved sugars from sources such as leaves to other parts that use or store them. Their functions are different but connected. Water reaching leaves can contribute to photosynthesis, while sugars produced in leaves can be moved to other organs. Comparing xylem and phloem shows how different tissues can have specialised roles while contributing to the same overall plant system.

A connected plant system

Water movement, gas exchange, sugar production and transport are linked across the plant. Roots absorb water and dissolved mineral ions. Xylem connects this supply with other parts of the plant. Leaves capture light and exchange gases, and photosynthesis forms sugars from carbon dioxide and water while releasing oxygen. Phloem then transports dissolved sugars from sources such as leaves to other parts of the plant that use or store them. Each process depends on structures and tissues performing their roles within the larger system.

Because these processes are connected, a change in one organ or tissue can influence other parts of the plant. For example, if a hypothetical plant had roots that absorbed less water, less water would be available for transport through the plant. This could affect processes in leaves that depend on water. Similarly, if gas exchange in leaves was reduced, the supply of carbon dioxide for photosynthesis could be affected. These examples show why scientists examine relationships between parts as well as the function of each part by itself.

Scientific representations can help communicate these relationships. A labelled diagram might show roots, a stem, leaves, xylem and phloem, with labels identifying their functions or connections. A comparison can also link a structure directly to what it does, such as an extended root-hair cell surface supporting absorption or many chloroplasts in palisade cells supporting photosynthesis. Representations are useful, but they simplify real three-dimensional plant systems. A clear scientific explanation should therefore use the representation as a communication tool while recognising that the real plant contains many connected organs, tissues and specialised cells.

M02-S03

Ecosystems, energy, populations and extinction

Relationships in ecosystems

An ecosystem includes living organisms and the non-living parts of their environment. The living parts are called biotic factors and include plants, animals, fungi and microorganisms. The non-living parts are called abiotic factors and include conditions such as light, temperature, water and the physical surroundings. Organisms depend on both biotic and abiotic factors, so a change in one part of an ecosystem can affect other parts.

Organisms also have different roles in feeding relationships. Producers make their own food using energy from their surroundings, while consumers obtain energy by eating other organisms. Decomposers break down dead organisms and wastes, returning matter to the environment where it can be reused. These interactions connect organisms into systems rather than separate, independent groups.

A useful way to understand an ecosystem is to look for relationships rather than studying one organism alone. For example, a change in water availability could affect producers, which may then affect the consumers that depend on them. Scientists use observations and data about these relationships to explain how changes can spread through an ecosystem.

Food webs and energy flow

A food chain shows one possible pathway of feeding relationships, while a food web shows several connected pathways in the same ecosystem. Arrows in these representations show the direction in which energy is transferred from one organism to another. Because organisms can have more than one food source or predator, a food web usually gives a more complete picture of ecosystem relationships than a single food chain.

Energy enters feeding relationships through producers and is transferred to consumers when organisms are eaten. At each transfer, less energy remains available to the next feeding level because organisms use energy for life processes and some energy is transferred to the surroundings. An energy pyramid represents this pattern by showing more available energy near the producer level and less at higher feeding levels. The size of each level can be compared without assuming an exact percentage unless reliable data are provided.

Energy flows through an ecosystem rather than cycling back to its starting point. Matter behaves differently because materials can be reused through processes such as decomposition. When interpreting a food web or energy pyramid, it is useful to identify the producer level, trace possible energy pathways and consider how a change in one population could affect organisms connected to it.

Populations, change and extinction

A population is a group of organisms of the same species living in a particular area. Population size can change over time as environmental conditions and interactions change. Limiting factors are conditions that restrict population growth. They can include the availability of food, water or suitable habitat, as well as competition, predation, disease and changes in abiotic conditions.

Population data can show increases, decreases or periods of relative stability. A pattern in a graph is evidence that a change occurred, but the graph alone may not prove why it happened. A strong scientific conclusion describes the pattern shown by the data and then links possible causes to additional evidence about the ecosystem. For example, if a consumer population falls after its main food source declines, the feeding relationship could help explain the pattern, but the conclusion should match the evidence available.

Australia has experienced high rates of species population decline and extinction. These changes can involve several interacting pressures rather than one single cause. Contributing pressures can include habitat loss or change and fragmentation, introduced species, changed disturbance patterns, pollution, overexploitation and climate-related pressures. For example, habitat fragmentation may reduce access to food or shelter while an introduced predator adds another pressure, and climate-related changes may alter environmental conditions at the same time. Scientists therefore compare different types of evidence before deciding which factors are contributing to a decline.

Extinction occurs when no living members of a species remain. Extinction can result from interacting causes such as major habitat change, loss of food resources, environmental change, competition or other pressures. Its consequences can extend through food webs because other organisms may have depended on the extinct species for food, competition, decomposition or another ecological relationship. Explanations of extinction should therefore be based on evidence and should consider interacting pressures rather than assuming that one cause always explains the change.

M03-S01

Classification, properties and uses of matter

Classifying elements, compounds and mixtures

Matter can be classified by considering what it is made from. An element contains only one type of atom. A compound contains atoms of two or more different elements chemically joined together. Elements and compounds are both pure substances because each has a consistent composition.

A mixture contains two or more substances together without those substances being chemically joined into one new substance. The substances in a mixture keep their own chemical identities, and the proportions can vary. A description of a sample can therefore provide useful evidence for classification. If a sample contains only one type of atom it is an element; if different elements are chemically joined in a pure substance it is a compound; and if different substances are present together it is a mixture.

Classification should be based on evidence rather than appearance alone. Two samples may look similar but belong to different categories. Scientists compare information about composition and properties so that the classification matches the evidence provided.

Physical properties and broad material groups

Physical properties are features that can be observed or described without changing a substance into a different substance. Useful physical properties can include electrical conductivity, thermal conductivity, hardness, flexibility, brittleness, lustre and whether a material can be shaped. These properties can help scientists compare materials and decide which differences are important for a particular purpose.

Elements are often discussed using the broad groups metals, non-metals and metalloids. Many metals conduct electricity and heat well, have a shiny appearance when freshly exposed, and can often be shaped without breaking. Many non-metals are poorer conductors and, when solid, may be more brittle. Metalloids, such as silicon, have properties that are between or combine some tendencies of metals and non-metals. For example, silicon can conduct electricity under some conditions but not as freely as typical conducting metals. These are broad patterns rather than universal rules, so the properties of the actual element should be considered.

Common elements and compounds are used when their relevant properties suit a purpose and suitable sources are available. Copper is an element and metal whose electrical conductivity and ability to be drawn into wires support its use in electrical wiring. Silicon is an element commonly classified as a metalloid; it occurs in many minerals, and specially processed silicon is useful in electronic components because its electrical behaviour can be controlled. Silicon dioxide is a common compound found in materials such as quartz and sand and is an important raw material in glass production. Availability alone does not make a substance suitable, and a useful property alone does not guarantee that a material is practical to use. Both availability and relevant properties can influence material choices.

Using evidence to select a material

Choosing a material for a purpose means matching its properties to the needs of that use. For example, a material intended to carry an electric current would need suitable electrical conductivity, while a material intended to resist bending might need suitable strength or stiffness. The important property depends on what the material is expected to do.

A good recommendation uses the supplied evidence directly. If several materials are described, first identify the properties that matter for the stated use, then compare the materials using those properties. Availability can also be relevant. If two materials have suitable properties, information about how readily each can be sourced or processed may help explain why one is more commonly used. It is also useful to recognise limitations because a material may have one desirable property but another property that makes it less suitable.

An evidence-based conclusion should name the recommended material and explain why its properties and any relevant availability information make it the best choice from the options provided. The conclusion should not claim that the material is best for every situation. It should stay connected to the particular purpose and to the evidence available.

M03-S02

Atomic structure and changing models

Inside an atom

An atom is the smallest unit of an element that retains that element's chemical properties. In a simple atomic model, a small central nucleus contains protons and neutrons, while electrons are represented outside the nucleus. Protons have a positive relative charge, neutrons have no charge, and electrons have a negative relative charge.

The atomic number of an element is the number of protons in the nucleus of each atom of that element. Proton number therefore identifies the element. Changing the number of protons would mean the atom belongs to a different element. A neutral atom has equal numbers of protons and electrons, so their positive and negative charges balance overall.

Protons and neutrons each have a relative mass of approximately one relative mass unit. An electron has a much smaller mass and is commonly treated as having negligible mass in a simple comparison. The number of neutrons does not determine the element's identity, although neutrons are an important part of the nucleus.

Reading simple atomic models

Simple shell-style diagrams can help organise information about atomic structure. They usually place the nucleus in the centre and arrange electron symbols in regions around it. From a labelled diagram, you can identify protons, neutrons and electrons, compare their charges and relative masses, and use the proton number to determine the atomic number.

These diagrams are useful representations, but they are not literal pictures of atoms. The sizes and distances shown are not drawn to true scale, and electrons should not be imagined as tiny objects travelling along the fixed circular lines drawn on the page. The lines are a simplified way of organising information about electrons.

When interpreting an atomic model, separate what the model is designed to communicate from what it leaves out. A useful model may clearly show particle types and their arrangement while simplifying features that are more complex in actual atoms.

Why scientific models change

Scientific models are evidence-based simplifications used to describe, explain or predict features of the natural world. A model does not need to copy reality exactly to be useful. Instead, it should represent important features clearly enough to help scientists develop and test explanations.

New technologies can make new kinds of observations possible or provide evidence that was not previously available. When this evidence shows that an earlier model has important limitations, scientists can revise or replace the model so that it better explains the observations. This connection between improved technology, new evidence and model revision is one way scientific knowledge develops.

Atomic models are a good example of this process. As technologies enabled scientists to gather new evidence about atoms, atomic models were revised to account for the observations. A simple shell-style model remains useful for introducing the nucleus, subatomic particles and electron arrangements, but it does not show every feature of atomic behaviour. Recognising both what a model explains well and what it simplifies is an important part of using scientific models.

M03-S03

Periodic patterns, symbols and the first 18 elements

Reading element symbols and atomic numbers

Each element has a chemical symbol that provides a short way to identify it. A symbol contains one or two letters. The first letter is always a capital letter, and a second letter, when present, is lowercase. For example, H and He are different symbols, and Na represents one element rather than the separate symbols N and A. Correct capitalisation matters because it helps show where one element symbol ends and another begins.

Elements in the periodic table are arranged in order of atomic number. Atomic number is the number of protons in the nucleus of an atom, so it identifies the element. If two atoms have different proton numbers, they belong to different elements. This makes atomic number a reliable way to follow the order of elements in the periodic table.

When reading a periodic-table entry, the atomic number and symbol provide different but connected information. The symbol names the element in a compact form, while the atomic number identifies its proton number. Using both helps avoid relying only on an element's position or appearance.

Periods, groups and simple electron patterns

The horizontal rows of the periodic table are called periods, and the vertical columns are called groups. Across the first 18 elements, a simple shell-style model can be used to notice patterns in how electrons are arranged. In this simplified model, the first shell can hold up to two electrons, and for the first 18 elements the next shells are filled in a regular pattern that helps explain their positions. Moving across a period, the atomic number increases and electrons are added in a pattern within the same main occupied shell. Moving to a new period begins a new occupied shell, so elements in the same period have the same number of occupied electron shells in this simplified model.

Elements in the same group may share patterns in their outer electron arrangements and may also show some similar properties and reactivity. Within the first 18 elements, some groups contain elements with broadly similar chemical behaviour, while other elements differ in important ways. Periodic position can therefore be used to make cautious predictions about an unfamiliar element's likely properties or reactivity by comparing it with nearby elements or other members of its group. These are evidence-based predictions rather than guarantees, so the periodic table should not be treated as a rule that every element in a group behaves identically.

The periodic table was not produced in its present form all at once. As evidence about elements and their properties improved, scientists changed how elements were arranged and grouped so that recurring patterns were represented more consistently. Improved knowledge, including the use of atomic number to order elements, helped refine the table. This history shows that scientific models and classifications can change when stronger evidence provides a better explanation of observed patterns.

From element symbols to chemical formulas

An element symbol identifies one type of element, while a chemical formula can contain one or more element symbols. To interpret a simple formula, first identify each capital letter because it begins a new element symbol. If a lowercase letter follows, it belongs to the same symbol. For example, He is one element symbol, while H and O in H2O represent two different element types.

A formula can contain more than one atom while still representing only one element type. For example, O2 contains only the symbol O, so it contains one element type. A formula such as CO2 contains the symbols C and O, so it contains two element types. At this level, the important task is to recognise the symbols and count the different element types represented rather than explain how or why the atoms are joined.

Careful reading of capital and lowercase letters prevents mistakes. Co represents one element symbol, while C and O written separately represent two different element symbols. Using symbol rules together with the periodic table allows formulas to be interpreted consistently.

M04-S01

Energy transfers, stores and systems

Systems and energy stores

When scientists investigate energy changes, they can define a system as the object or group of objects being studied. Everything outside that chosen system is called the surroundings. Clearly identifying the system helps show where energy is stored, where it is transferred and whether energy or matter crosses the system boundary.

An open system can exchange both energy and matter with its surroundings. A closed system can exchange energy with its surroundings but is treated as not exchanging matter across its chosen boundary. These are useful models for tracking changes. A closed system is not necessarily isolated, and a classroom system should not be assumed to prevent all energy transfer.

Energy can be described using different stores. Chemical energy can be associated with substances such as fuels or food, kinetic energy with moving objects, gravitational potential energy with objects positioned in a gravitational field, elastic energy with stretched or compressed objects, and thermal energy with the internal energy associated with the temperature of matter. A system can involve more than one energy store at the same time.

Energy transfers and transformations

Energy can be transferred from one part of a system to another or between a system and its surroundings. Thermal energy can be transferred by conduction, convection or radiation. Conduction transfers thermal energy through interactions between neighbouring particles, without the material moving from one place to another overall. Convection transfers thermal energy through the movement of liquids or gases. Radiation transfers energy by electromagnetic waves and does not require matter to travel through.

Other useful descriptions of transfer pathways include mechanical work when forces cause movement and electrical transfer through an electrical interaction. An energy transformation describes a change from one energy store to another. For example, a decrease in a chemical energy store may be linked with increases in kinetic and thermal energy stores. A diagram can show these changes with labelled boxes and clearly directed arrows.

Energy is conserved during transfers and transformations. This means energy is not created or destroyed. Instead, energy can change stores, move between parts of a system or cross a system boundary. Defining whether a system is open or closed helps scientists keep track of which transfers of energy and matter need to be included in their explanation.

Useful energy and energy spread

In many systems, not all transferred energy remains useful for the intended purpose. Some energy spreads into the surroundings, often increasing thermal energy stores. Thermal energy may move through materials by conduction, through moving liquids or gases by convection, or by radiation. Once energy has become more widely spread through the surroundings, it can be harder to use for the original purpose. This does not mean the energy has disappeared or been destroyed.

A qualitative energy-system diagram can help track these changes without using exact measurements. A boundary can show what has been included in the system, while arrows can show energy transfers across or within that boundary. Labels can identify energy stores and pathways such as conduction, convection or radiation. A diagram may also compare an open system, where matter and energy can cross the boundary, with a closed system, where energy can cross but matter is treated as remaining within the boundary.

When reading an energy representation, first identify the system, surroundings and boundary. Decide whether the representation treats the system as open or closed, then trace the arrows and identify the relevant energy stores and transfer mechanisms. Finally, check whether the explanation accounts for energy transferred to the surroundings rather than treating that energy as missing. Visual features should only be given a special meaning when the diagram or its legend explains that meaning.

M04-S02

Physical and chemical change

Physical and chemical changes

In a physical change, the substances present remain the same even though their form, state or arrangement may change. Changes of state, changes in shape and some mixing processes can be described as physical changes when no new substance is formed. The important question is not simply whether something looks different, but whether the substances themselves have changed.

In a chemical change, one or more new substances are formed. The starting substances are called reactants and the new substances formed are called products. Deciding whether a change is physical or chemical requires evidence about what happened to the substances, rather than relying only on whether the change appears large, dramatic or difficult to reverse.

Chemical reactions also involve energy changes. Energy can be transferred from the surroundings to the reacting system or from the reacting system to the surroundings. These energy transfers can occur in different forms, so a reaction should not be classified only by whether it seems to become warmer or cooler.

Using observations and word equations

Observations can provide evidence that a chemical change may have occurred. These can include gas formation, an unexpected colour change, a temperature change or the formation of a new solid called a precipitate. However, no single observation automatically proves that a new substance has formed. For example, a temperature change could have another explanation, so scientists consider several observations and the context together.

A qualitative word equation summarises a chemical change using the names of the reactants and products. Reactant names are written before an arrow and product names after the arrow, such as reactant A + reactant B → product C. The arrow means that the reactants change to form the products; it does not show a practical procedure or explain every detail of how the change occurs.

Photosynthesis and cellular respiration are examples of chemical processes in living things. A qualitative relationship for photosynthesis is carbon dioxide + water → glucose + oxygen. Energy from light is transferred into this process and becomes associated with the chemical substances produced. A qualitative relationship for cellular respiration is glucose + oxygen → carbon dioxide + water. During cellular respiration, energy associated with the reacting substances is transferred and can support processes in living cells, with some energy also transferred to the surroundings. These word relationships summarise overall chemical changes rather than every step involved.

Conservation and judging evidence

During a chemical change, atoms are rearranged into new combinations, but the atoms themselves are conserved. Matter is therefore conserved. In a closed system, where matter does not enter or leave, the total mass before and after a chemical change is expected to remain the same even though the substances present may be different.

Strong scientific conclusions match the quality of the evidence. A useful reflection considers whether observations were clear and consistent, whether important information was missing, and whether another explanation could fit the same evidence. If the available data support a chemical change but do not prove it completely, the conclusion should state that level of certainty rather than make a stronger claim than the evidence allows.

M04-S03

Geological change, rocks, fossils and plate evidence

Geological change and the rock cycle

Earth materials can change over very different timescales. Weathering breaks rock into smaller pieces or changes it at Earth's surface, while erosion removes material and transport moves it from one place to another. Deposition occurs when transported material settles. Over time, these processes can contribute to the formation of new rocks and changes in existing rocks. Rocks and minerals can also be described and compared using visible properties such as colour, crystal or grain size, texture, lustre, transparency, layering and the presence of visible fossils. No single property always identifies a material, so several observations are more useful than one alone.

The rock cycle is a model that connects igneous, sedimentary and metamorphic rocks. Igneous rocks form from cooled molten material, sedimentary rocks can form from accumulated sediments, and metamorphic rocks form when existing rocks are changed without completely melting. The cycle is not a single fixed pathway: rocks can follow different routes, and a diagram simplifies the much more complex geological changes that occur in Earth. Elemental-composition comparisons also depend on what part of a planet is being compared. For example, Earth's whole-planet composition includes large amounts of iron, oxygen, silicon and magnesium, while Earth's crust is especially rich in oxygen and silicon. Mars surface rocks and dust also contain elements such as oxygen, silicon and iron, but their relative amounts and geological setting differ. A whole-planet comparison should therefore not be treated as the same as a crust or surface comparison.

Fossils, rock layers and relative age

Fossils and rock layers provide evidence about past environments and the sequence of geological events. Fossils are preserved remains, traces or evidence of past organisms. Sedimentary rock layers can also record changes through time. In a sequence that has not been overturned or greatly disturbed, lower layers are generally older than layers above them, so their positions can support conclusions about relative age.

Relative age tells which rock, fossil or event is older or younger compared with another; it does not automatically give an exact numerical age. Fossils found in particular layers can help scientists compare sequences and develop explanations about past conditions. Conclusions are stronger when several pieces of geological evidence agree, and they should remain cautious if layers are missing, disturbed or difficult to compare. Evidence about geological events can also be preserved in authorised Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander Cultural accounts passed across generations. Where such accounts are used in learning, they need appropriate permission, source context and respectful attribution. A local Cultural account should not be invented, copied, retold or attributed without the authority and context needed to share it.

Evidence for moving plates

Scientists use several lines of evidence to explain that Earth's plates have moved over time. Matching patterns of fossils and rocks on land areas that are now separated can support the idea that those areas were once connected differently. The shapes of continental margins can also suggest a possible fit, although shape alone is not enough to establish a strong explanation. Plate movement also changes Earth's crust and surface. Where plates interact, stress can build and be released as earthquakes, while molten rock can sometimes rise and produce volcanic activity. The way many earthquakes and volcanoes are concentrated around plate boundaries provides further evidence of active crustal change and moving plates.

Multiple independent lines of evidence provide stronger support than one observation by itself. A geological model can combine rock patterns, fossil evidence, earthquake and volcano patterns and the relative positions of land areas to help explain past or continuing plate movement. Authorised Cultural accounts describing earthquakes or volcanic events can also contribute evidence about changes remembered across generations when they are used with appropriate permission and context. Like all scientific models, a geological model simplifies reality, so its usefulness depends on what evidence it represents clearly and what details it leaves out.

M05-S01

Scientific models and evidence

What scientific models represent

A scientific model is a representation of selected features or relationships in a system. Models can be diagrams, physical representations, mathematical relationships or computer-based representations. Scientists use models to describe how a system is organised, explain relationships between parts, or make predictions about what may happen under particular conditions.

A model may contain inputs, components, variables or parameters, relationships and outputs. A variable is a feature that can change, while a parameter is a value or condition used to define how part of the model behaves. Models also include assumptions, which are simplified statements or conditions accepted so the model can focus on particular features. These choices make a model useful, but they also mean the model is not the same as the real system.

Comparing models with evidence

Scientific models are developed using evidence, but evidence and models are different. Raw observations or measurements provide evidence about a system. A model uses selected evidence and relationships to represent or explain that system. Scientists can compare a model's output or prediction with observations to judge how well the model works for its intended purpose.

When observations agree with a model's prediction, they can support the model without proving that it is perfect. When observations do not agree, the evidence may challenge an assumption, relationship or other part of the model. Scientists can then investigate the difference and revise the model when the evidence supports a better representation or explanation.

Scientific inquiry is one way of investigating natural phenomena. It uses testable questions, systematic evidence and explanations that can be revised when new evidence becomes available. Other approaches to knowledge may have different purposes, traditions or bases for understanding. These approaches should not all be treated as the same, and a belief, tradition or opinion should not be presented as scientific evidence unless it has been investigated using scientific methods.

Limitations and appropriate use

Every scientific model has limitations because it represents selected features and leaves others out. A limitation might involve a simplified assumption, missing variable, restricted range of conditions or relationship that does not represent every situation. Identifying a limitation does not automatically make a model useless; it helps define where the model can be used appropriately.

A strong conclusion from a model stays within the evidence and the model's appropriate-use boundary. If a model represents one set of conditions, its prediction should not automatically be extended to very different conditions. Scientists therefore consider the model's purpose, assumptions, evidence and limitations before deciding how much confidence to place in an explanation or prediction.

M05-S02

Simulations, predictions and applications of models

How simulations represent systems

A simulation is a type of model that represents how parts of a system may change or interact. A simulation can include an input, one or more parameters, a rule or relationship, and an output. Inputs provide starting information, parameters set conditions that can be varied, and the model's rules describe how these features are related. The output is the result produced by the simulation.

Scientists can compare simulation runs by changing one parameter while keeping other model conditions the same. This can reveal how sensitive the output is to that parameter. The resulting pattern is a model output, not a direct observation of the real world, so it should be described as what the simulation predicts under its stated inputs, rules and assumptions.

Predictions depend on evidence and assumptions

A prediction from a simulation is conditional. It depends on the inputs, data, assumptions, parameters and relationships used by the model. If these change, the predicted output may also change. A prediction therefore describes what the model indicates under particular conditions rather than stating with certainty what must happen in the real world.

Scientists can evaluate a simulation by comparing its outputs with relevant observations. Agreement can support the model's usefulness for a particular purpose, while repeated differences may show that assumptions, inputs or relationships need closer examination. Repeating the same simulation with the same settings can also check whether it produces consistent outputs, but repeatable model output does not by itself prove that the model accurately represents reality.

Sensitivity, limitations and appropriate applications

A simulation is sensitive to a parameter when a change in that parameter produces a noticeable change in the output. Sensitivity can help identify which parts of a model strongly influence its predictions. It can also reveal a limitation: if a small change in an uncertain input produces very different outputs, conclusions based on the model may need to be cautious.

Models and simulations can support decisions when they are suitable for the question and used within their limits. Generic applications might include representing weather patterns, changes in an animal population or energy use in a system. A responsible evaluation considers the quality of the evidence, the assumptions, the model's purpose and any limitations before using a prediction to support a conclusion.

M05-S03

Five-hour depth-study portfolio

Building a focused evidence pathway

A strong depth study begins with a focused investigable question. The question should be clear enough to guide what evidence is needed and what relationships or patterns should be examined. Evidence can come from supplied or appropriately authorised datasets, reliable secondary sources, scientific models or simulations. The important step is to choose evidence that can genuinely help answer the question rather than collecting information simply because it is available.

A portfolio should preserve an evidence trail. Raw evidence is the information collected or supplied before it is reorganised for analysis, while a processed representation may summarise that evidence in a table, graph, category or other suitable form. Keeping a clear link between the original evidence and the processed representation makes it easier to check interpretations and explain where conclusions came from.

From evidence to analysis and conclusion

Analysis means looking for meaningful patterns, relationships, differences and anomalies in the evidence. An anomaly is a result or observation that does not follow the main pattern and may need closer consideration. Good analysis also considers uncertainty, missing information and whether a pattern is strong enough to support the claim being made. A graph or summary is useful only when the student explains what it shows.

The conclusion should directly answer the investigable question and be supported by the analysed evidence. It should not make a stronger claim than the evidence allows. If the evidence is limited or uncertain, the conclusion should acknowledge this rather than present an unsupported certainty. Evidence that does not fit the main pattern should be considered rather than ignored.

Reflection, improvement and source records

Reflection considers how trustworthy and useful the study's evidence and reasoning are. A useful reflection identifies limitations, such as incomplete data, uncertain assumptions, a restricted range of evidence or features that a model leaves out. It can then suggest a realistic improvement that would strengthen the evidence or make the conclusion more dependable without pretending that every limitation can be removed.

Sources should be acknowledged so the origin of information, data, models or ideas can be identified. A clear source record supports the evidence trail by showing where material came from and helping distinguish supplied evidence from the student's own interpretation. Together, the question, evidence record, processed representation, analysis, conclusion, reflection and source acknowledgement form a coherent scientific portfolio.
